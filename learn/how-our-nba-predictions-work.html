Explainer

How our NBA predictions work

Every prediction on Drop Coverage is minutes times production: how long we expect a player to be on the court tonight, multiplied by what he produces per minute, adjusted for the opponent, then given a range the result should land inside 80% of the time.

Minutes come first

Most of what changes from night to night is playing time, so that's where the model starts. A team plays 240 minutes a game (a little more on average, once overtime is counted), and those minutes are shared out. When a player is ruled out, his minutes are shared among the teammates still available. Once teams confirm their starting fives near tip-off, more of them go to the teammates who have absorbed that player's minutes before.

On top of each player's usual role, the model adjusts for the situations that reliably move minutes:

  • Back-to-backs. On the second night, starters play a little less and the bench a little more.
  • Coming back from an absence. A player returning after missing three to nine games is eased back in slightly; after ten or more, noticeably.
  • Expected blowouts. When the bookmakers' spread is ten points or more, the bench gets extra time.
  • Playoffs. Rotations shorten: starters play more, the bench less.
  • Who starts. In the morning we use the projected starting fives and the depth chart; once teams confirm their fives near tip-off, we use them. A player projected to start out of his usual position gets that position for the night.

Then production per minute

Each player's points, rebounds, assists and threes per minute come from his recent games, with older games counting for less. On the second night of a back-to-back, assists and threes per minute dip slightly. Rookies and anyone with fewer than ten NBA games get an early estimate that blends their own games with a typical player at their position, with wider ranges, and they're kept out of edges until they've played more.

Then the opponent

Some defences give up more to guards, others to big men. For each opponent we measure what it allows to each position against the league average, with recent games weighted more heavily, and apply that at half strength. Tested on last season, full strength helped late in the season and hurt early, when the numbers are thin; half strength held up in both. At the start of a season last season's regular-season games carry the number, fading as this season's build up (about 40% of the weight after two weeks, 80% by Christmas). Starting from scratch each October tested worse for the first month, even against the teams that changed most. Last season's playoff games are left out: short rotations and series against one opponent made the early numbers worse.

Then a range

A single number hides how uncertain a night is, so every prediction carries a range the result should land inside 80% of the time. The range isn't printed on the board; it's what turns a prediction and a line into a probability, and the track record reports how often results actually landed inside it. What the ranges mean explains how they're built.

With and without a star

When a star sits, two things change for his teammates: who plays his minutes, and who takes his shots. The model handles both. His minutes go to the teammates who have picked them up before. Each teammate's scoring rate then moves by how that player has scored per minute in their games without the star. That's checked against how many of his recent games already came without the star, so a rate built while covering for him isn't lifted twice, and it comes back down when the star returns. A pair with only a few games apart leans on how big a share of the team's shots the missing player takes, until it has a record of its own. The board's note ("Jokić out: +3.1 without him") shows the raw per-game difference. The prediction uses the tested version, which is smaller, because most of that difference is minutes already counted.

What isn't in the prediction

Some things look like they should matter and didn't survive testing. Home and away made almost no difference once minutes were right. We show what we can as information and leave the number alone until the evidence says otherwise.

Tested before it's used

Every piece above had to make predictions more accurate on games it hadn't seen (last season, split into early and late halves) before it went in. Ideas that looked good but made the numbers worse were left out. Even so, most of a miss is the night itself: with a player's exact minutes known in advance, points predictions would still miss by about four points a game on average.

When the numbers update

The main build runs early each morning Sydney time, after the afternoon injury report in the US. The numbers are rerun after each later injury report, 90 minutes before tip-off and once more just before it. When a prediction moves, the board shows the earlier number and why ("22.4 → 25.1, Jokić out").
